# Finance Dictionary / Glossary

A concise reference for retail investors, based on Corporate Finance Institute (CFI) definitions and explanations.

---

## A

### Alpha
Alpha measures the excess return of an investment relative to the return of a benchmark index. It is often interpreted as the value that a portfolio manager adds or subtracts from a fund's return. A positive alpha indicates outperformance; a negative alpha indicates underperformance.

### Amortization
Amortization is the process of paying off debt with a fixed repayment schedule in regular installments over time. In accounting, it also refers to expensing the cost of an intangible asset over its useful life. It is similar to depreciation, but applies to intangible assets rather than physical assets.

### ATR (Average True Range)
Average True Range is a technical analysis indicator that measures market volatility by decomposing the entire range of an asset price for that period. A higher ATR indicates higher volatility, while a lower ATR suggests lower volatility. Traders often use ATR to set stop-loss levels or position sizes.

---

## B

### Balance Sheet
The balance sheet is a financial statement that reports a company's assets, liabilities, and shareholders' equity at a specific point in time. It provides a snapshot of what the company owns, what it owes, and the value attributable to shareholders. The balance sheet follows the equation: Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity.

### Beta
Beta measures the systematic risk or volatility of a stock or portfolio in relation to the overall market. A beta greater than 1 indicates the asset is more volatile than the market; less than 1 means it is less volatile. A beta of 0 implies no correlation with the market, while a negative beta indicates an inverse relationship.

### Bollinger Bands
Bollinger Bands consist of a middle band being a simple moving average and two outer bands plotted two standard deviations above and below the middle band. They help traders identify overbought and oversold conditions by measuring price volatility. When the bands widen, volatility is increasing; when they contract, volatility is decreasing.

## C

### Capex
Capital expenditures, or capex, are funds used by a company to acquire, upgrade, and maintain physical assets such as property, plants, and equipment. These expenditures are capitalized on the balance sheet and depreciated over the useful life of the asset. Capex is a key indicator of a company's investment in future growth and operational capacity.

### CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model)
CAPM describes the relationship between systematic risk and expected return for assets, particularly stocks. The model asserts that the expected return of a stock equals the risk-free rate plus a risk premium based on the stock's beta. It is widely used to estimate the cost of equity and to determine whether an investment offers a reasonable expected return for its risk.

### Capital Structure
Capital structure refers to the mix of debt and equity that a company uses to finance its operations and growth. An optimal capital structure minimizes the company's cost of capital while maximizing shareholder value. Too much debt increases financial risk, while too much equity can dilute ownership and raise the cost of capital.

### Cash Flow Statement
The cash flow statement reports the cash generated and spent during a specific accounting period. It is divided into three sections: operating activities, investing activities, and financing activities. This statement reveals how a company manages its cash, funds its operations, and pays its debts.

### Coefficient of Determination (R-squared)
R-squared measures the proportion of the variance in a dependent variable that is predictable from independent variable(s). In investing, it indicates how closely the returns of a fund track its benchmark index. An R-squared of 100% means the fund's movements are entirely explained by its benchmark; lower values indicate less correlation.

### Correlation
Correlation measures the statistical relationship between two variables, such as the returns of two different assets. Values range from -1 (perfect negative correlation) to +1 (perfect positive correlation), with 0 indicating no linear relationship. Diversification benefits often come from combining assets with low or negative correlations.

### Cost of Debt
The cost of debt is the effective interest rate that a company pays on its borrowed funds. It is calculated as the average interest rate on all outstanding debt, adjusted for the tax shield since interest payments are tax-deductible. The after-tax cost of debt is a key input for calculating the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC).

### Cost of Equity
The cost of equity is the return required by equity investors for investing in a company, compensating them for the risk of ownership. It is commonly estimated using CAPM or the dividend growth model. Because equity holders are last in line during liquidation, the cost of equity is typically higher than the cost of debt.

### Covariance
Covariance measures the directional relationship between the returns of two assets. A positive covariance means the assets tend to move in the same direction, while a negative covariance means they move oppositely. Covariance is used in modern portfolio theory to help construct diversified portfolios that minimize risk.

### CVaR
Conditional Value at Risk, or CVaR, estimates the expected loss in the worst-case scenarios beyond the VaR threshold. For example, a 95% CVaR measures the average loss in the most extreme 5% of cases. CVaR provides more information than VaR because it accounts for the severity of tail losses, making it useful for stress testing and regulatory risk frameworks.

## D

### DCF (Discounted Cash Flow)
Discounted Cash Flow is a valuation method that estimates the value of an investment based on its expected future cash flows, discounted back to present value using a discount rate. The core premise is that an asset is worth the sum of all future cash it can generate. DCF analysis is widely used for intrinsic valuation of companies, projects, and other assets.

### Depreciation
Depreciation is the accounting method of allocating the cost of a tangible asset over its useful life. It reflects the wear and tear, obsolescence, or usage of physical assets such as machinery, buildings, and vehicles. Common methods include straight-line and declining balance depreciation.

### Discount Rate
The discount rate is the interest rate used to convert future cash flows into their present value in a DCF analysis. It reflects the time value of money and the risk associated with the investment. For companies, the discount rate is often the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC).

### Dividend Discount Model (DDM)
The Dividend Discount Model values a stock by calculating the present value of all future dividend payments. It is based on the principle that the intrinsic value of a stock is the sum of its expected future dividends, discounted back at the required rate of return. The simplicity of DDM makes it most suitable for companies with stable, predictable dividend policies.

### Dividend Yield
Dividend yield is the annual dividend payment per share divided by the stock's current share price, expressed as a percentage. It shows the cash return an investor receives from dividends relative to the stock price. Income-focused investors rely on dividend yield, though an unusually high yield may signal a falling stock price or an unsustainable payout.

### Downside Capture
Downside Capture measures how much a fund or portfolio lost relative to its benchmark during periods when the benchmark had negative returns. A downside capture ratio below 100% means the fund held up better than the benchmark in down markets. Investors focused on capital preservation often prioritize funds with low downside capture.

### Downside Deviation
Downside deviation measures the dispersion of returns that fall below a minimum acceptable return, typically the risk-free rate or zero. It penalizes only harmful volatility, ignoring upside surprises, and is the denominator in the Sortino ratio. Lower downside deviation means fewer and less severe negative returns.

### Drawdown
Drawdown refers to the decline from a peak to a trough in the value of an investment or trading account, usually expressed as a percentage. It measures downside risk and helps investors understand the potential pain of holding an asset. Max drawdown is the largest peak-to-trough loss suffered during a given period.

## E

### EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization is a measure of a company's overall operating performance. EBITDA removes non-cash expenses and capital structure effects to show profitability from core operations. While useful for comparison, it can be misleading because it ignores real costs like maintenance capex and working capital needs.

### EBIT
Earnings Before Interest and Taxes, also known as operating income, measures a company's profit from its core operations before paying interest and taxes. It excludes financing and tax expenses, making it useful for comparing operating performance across companies with different capital structures and tax rates. EBIT is a key component of EBITDA calculations.

### EBITDA Margin
EBITDA margin is EBITDA divided by total revenue, expressed as a percentage. It shows how much cash profit a company generates from its revenue before capital structure and tax considerations. Higher margins generally indicate stronger operational efficiency, but comparisons are most meaningful within the same industry.

### Enterprise Value (EV)
Enterprise Value represents the total value of a company, including both equity and debt, minus cash and cash equivalents. It is calculated as market capitalization plus total debt minus cash. EV is useful for comparing companies with different capital structures and for calculating valuation multiples such as EV/EBITDA.

### Equity Risk Premium (ERP)
The equity risk premium is the excess return that investing in stocks provides over the risk-free rate. It compensates investors for taking on the higher risk of equities compared to risk-free assets like government bonds. ERP is a fundamental input in CAPM for estimating the cost of equity.

### EV/EBITDA
Enterprise Value to EBITDA is a valuation multiple that compares a company's enterprise value to its operating cash flow proxy. It is widely used because it is capital-structure neutral and eliminates the effects of depreciation and amortization policies. Lower EV/EBITDA multiples may indicate undervaluation, though industry norms vary significantly.

### EV/EBIT
Enterprise Value to EBIT is similar to EV/EBITDA but includes depreciation and amortization in the denominator. It is useful for capital-intensive industries where depreciation is a significant operating expense. This multiple allows investors to compare companies with different asset ages and depreciation methods.

### EV/Sales
Enterprise Value to Sales compares a company's enterprise value to its revenue. It is particularly useful for valuing loss-making or low-margin companies where earnings-based multiples may be negative or distorted. Like other EV multiples, it is independent of capital structure, making peer comparisons cleaner.

## F

### Factor Investing
Factor investing is an investment strategy that targets specific drivers of return, known as factors, such as value, momentum, size, quality, and low volatility. The approach is rooted in academic research showing that certain characteristics are persistently rewarded in markets. Factor investing aims to capture these systematic sources of excess return over a broad market portfolio.

### Factor Models
Factor models are statistical tools used to explain the returns of securities or portfolios by decomposing them into exposures to various risk factors. The most well-known is the Fama-French three-factor model, which extends CAPM by adding size and value factors. These models help investors understand what drives portfolio returns and whether returns are due to skill or factor exposure.

### Fama-French
The Fama-French model is a multi-factor model developed by Eugene Fama and Kenneth French that explains stock returns using three risk factors: market risk, size (small minus big), and value (high minus low book-to-market). It expanded on CAPM by showing that small-cap and value stocks historically outperform large-cap and growth stocks. The model later evolved into a five-factor model with profitability and investment factors.

### FCFF (Free Cash Flow to Firm)
Free Cash Flow to Firm represents the cash available to all investors—both debt holders and equity holders—after operating expenses, taxes, and necessary capital expenditures. FCFF is calculated from operating income and is used in enterprise-level DCF valuation. It is independent of capital structure, making it ideal for cross-company comparison.

### FCFE (Free Cash Flow to Equity)
Free Cash Flow to Equity is the cash available to equity shareholders after all expenses, reinvestment, and debt payments have been accounted for. It is derived from FCFF by subtracting net debt payments and interest expenses (after tax). FCFE is used for equity valuation when a company's leverage is expected to change significantly over time.

### Free Cash Flow (FCF)
Free Cash Flow is the cash a company generates after accounting for cash outflows to support operations and maintain its capital assets. It is commonly calculated as operating cash flow minus capital expenditures, and represents the discretionary cash available to all investors. FCF is a key metric for assessing a company's ability to pursue opportunities, pay dividends, buy back shares, or reduce debt.

### Float
Float refers to the number of shares available for trading by the public, calculated as outstanding shares minus restricted shares held by insiders and institutions. A lower float can lead to higher volatility because fewer shares are available to absorb large buy or sell orders. Float is an important metric when assessing liquidity and potential price swings.

## G

### Gordon Growth Model
The Gordon Growth Model is a DDM variant that values a stock with dividends growing at a constant rate forever. It calculates the present value of an infinite series of dividend payments growing at a steady rate. The model is extremely sensitive to the growth and discount rate assumptions, and works best for mature companies with stable dividend growth.

### Gross Margin
Gross margin is revenue minus cost of goods sold, divided by revenue, expressed as a percentage. It represents the percentage of each dollar of revenue that the company retains as gross profit after producing its goods or services. Higher gross margins indicate better pricing power or lower production costs relative to competitors.

---

## I

### Income Statement
The income statement, or profit and loss (P&L) statement, shows a company's revenues, expenses, and net income or loss over a specific accounting period. It reveals how much profit or loss a business generated from its operations. The bottom line, net income, is useful for assessing profitability and is a key input for valuation multiples.

### Insider Ownership
Insider ownership refers to the percentage of a company's outstanding shares owned by insiders such as executives, directors, and large shareholders. High insider ownership can align management interests with those of public shareholders, signaling confidence in the company's prospects. Conversely, low insider ownership may suggest weaker alignment or limited skin in the game.

### Information Ratio
The Information Ratio measures the active return of a portfolio relative to the return of a benchmark, divided by the tracking error. It indicates how much excess return a manager generates per unit of additional risk taken versus the benchmark. A higher information ratio implies better risk-adjusted performance relative to the benchmark.

### IRR (Internal Rate of Return)
IRR is the discount rate at which the net present value (NPV) of all future cash flows from an investment equals zero. It represents the annualized effective return that the project or investment is expected to generate. Investors compare IRR to their required rate of return or cost of capital to decide whether to proceed with an investment.

---

## L

### LBO (Leveraged Buyout)
A Leveraged Buyout is the acquisition of a company using a significant amount of borrowed money to meet the cost of acquisition. The assets of the target company are often used as collateral for the loans, and the acquirer contributes a relatively small equity stake. LBOs are frequently executed by private equity firms seeking to enhance returns through leverage and operational improvements.

## M

### MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence)
MACD is a trend-following momentum indicator that shows the relationship between two moving averages of a security's price. It is calculated by subtracting the 26-period Exponential Moving Average (EMA) from the 12-period EMA, and a nine-day EMA called the signal line is plotted on top. Traders watch for crossovers and divergences to spot potential buy or sell signals.

### Market Cap
Market capitalization is the total market value of a company's outstanding shares, calculated as share price multiplied by shares outstanding. It classifies companies into categories such as large-cap, mid-cap, and small-cap. Market cap is a simple measure of a company's size and is widely used for indexing and portfolio construction.

### Max Drawdown
Max drawdown is the maximum loss from a peak to a trough of a portfolio or investment before a new peak is achieved. It measures the worst historical loss an investor could have experienced and is a key indicator of downside risk. Max drawdown is often preferred over standard deviation when assessing the tail risks of an investment strategy.

### M&A (Mergers & Acquisitions)
Mergers and Acquisitions refer to the consolidation of companies or assets through various types of financial transactions. Mergers involve two companies combining to form a new entity, while acquisitions occur when one company purchases another. M&A activity is driven by strategic goals such as growth, diversification, cost synergies, or market share expansion.

### Momentum Factor
The momentum factor targets stocks that have had strong recent performance, based on the empirical tendency of winners to keep winning and losers to keep losing over intermediate horizons. Momentum strategies buy recent outperformers and sell underperformers, typically over three to twelve-month holding periods. The premium is attributed to persistent behavioral biases such as investor underreaction and delayed confirmation of good news.

### Moving Average
A moving average is a technical indicator that smooths out price data by creating a constantly updated average price over a specific time period. Common types include Simple Moving Average (SMA) and Exponential Moving Average (EMA). Moving averages help traders identify trends and dynamic support or resistance levels.

## N

### Net Margin
Net margin is net income divided by revenue, expressed as a percentage. It represents the percentage of revenue remaining after all expenses, taxes, interest, and costs have been deducted. High net margins indicate strong overall profitability and cost control relative to competitors.

### Net Working Capital (NWC)
Net Working Capital equals current assets minus current liabilities and measures a company's short-term liquidity and operational efficiency. Positive NWC indicates the company has enough short-term assets to cover its short-term obligations. Changes in NWC are a critical component of free cash flow calculations.

---

## O

### Operating Margin
Operating margin is operating income, or EBIT, divided by revenue. It shows the percentage of revenue remaining after paying for variable costs of production but before interest and taxes. This margin highlights how efficiently a company generates profit from its core operations, excluding financing and tax effects.

---

## P

### P/B (Price-to-Book)
The Price-to-Book ratio compares a company's market capitalization to its book value, or equivalently, its share price to its book value per share. It indicates how much investors are willing to pay for each dollar of net assets. P/B is especially useful for valuing asset-heavy businesses and identifying potentially undervalued stocks.

### P/CF (Price-to-Cash Flow)
Price-to-Cash Flow compares a company's market price per share to its operating cash flow per share. Unlike earnings, cash flow is harder to manipulate through accounting choices, making P/CF a conservative valuation metric. It is particularly useful for capital-intensive companies or those with large non-cash expenses.

### Payout Ratio
The payout ratio is the proportion of earnings paid out as dividends to shareholders, expressed as a percentage. It indicates how sustainably a company's dividend is covered by its earnings. A very high payout ratio may signal limited retained earnings for reinvestment, while a low ratio suggests greater retention for growth.

### PEG Ratio
The Price/Earnings-to-Growth ratio compares a stock's P/E ratio to its expected earnings growth rate. It helps investors determine whether a high-growth stock is overvalued relative to its growth prospects. A lower PEG ratio may suggest a stock is undervalued given its growth rate.

### P/E (Price-to-Earnings)
The Price-to-Earnings ratio compares a company's share price to its earnings per share. It shows how much investors are willing to pay for one dollar of current or future earnings. High P/E ratios often indicate growth expectations, while low P/E ratios may signal undervaluation or underlying problems.

### P/S (Price-to-Sales)
The Price-to-Sales ratio compares a company's market capitalization to its revenue. It is useful for valuing early-stage or unprofitable companies where earnings may be negative or erratic. P/S is also valuable for comparing companies with different depreciation and amortization policies.

---

## Q

### Quality Factor
The quality factor refers to stocks with high profitability, low leverage, stable earnings, and strong governance. Quality-oriented strategies seek to overweight these companies, which have historically delivered superior risk-adjusted returns over time. Quality is measured through metrics such as high return on equity, low debt, and consistent cash flow generation.

---

## R

### Regression
Regression is a statistical method used to estimate the relationships among variables, most commonly to understand how one variable changes with another. In finance, linear regression is the foundation of the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), which relates an asset's returns to market returns. Investors use regression to quantify sensitivity, such as a stock's beta, and to test factor exposures in multi-factor models.

### Retention Ratio
The retention ratio is the proportion of net income retained by a company rather than paid out as dividends. It is calculated as one minus the payout ratio. A higher retention ratio means more earnings are reinvested into the business, which can fuel future growth if capital is deployed effectively.

### Return on Assets (ROA)
Return on Assets measures how efficiently a company's management uses its assets to generate earnings, calculated as net income divided by total assets. It answers the question: how much profit is the company squeezing from every dollar of assets? ROA is most useful when comparing companies within the same industry.

### Return on Equity (ROE)
Return on Equity measures a company's profitability relative to shareholders' book equity, calculated as net income divided by shareholders' equity. It reveals how effectively management is using investors' capital to generate earnings growth. High and consistent ROE is often a hallmark of high-quality businesses.

### Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)
Return on Invested Capital measures the return a company earns on the capital invested in its operations, including debt and equity. It is calculated as operating income after tax divided by invested capital. ROIC greater than the company's cost of capital indicates value creation, while ROIC below cost of capital signals value destruction.

### Risk-Free Rate
The risk-free rate is the theoretical return of an investment with zero risk of financial loss, typically represented by yields on short-term government bonds from stable countries. It serves as the foundation for pricing risky assets and is a key component of models such as CAPM. In practice, investors use treasury bill yields as a proxy for the risk-free rate over short horizons.

### RSI (Relative Strength Index)
RSI is a momentum oscillator that measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes to evaluate overbought or oversold conditions in the price of a stock or other asset. It ranges from 0 to 100, with traditional thresholds of 70 for overbought and 30 for oversold. Divergences between RSI and price can signal potential trend reversals.

### R-squared
R-squared measures the proportion of the variance in a dependent variable that is predictable from independent variable(s). In investing, it indicates how closely the returns of a fund track its benchmark index. An R-squared of 100% means the fund's movements are entirely explained by its benchmark; lower values indicate less correlation.

## S

### Sharpe Ratio
The Sharpe Ratio measures the risk-adjusted return of a portfolio by calculating excess return per unit of total volatility. It is computed as the portfolio return minus the risk-free rate, divided by the standard deviation of returns. A higher Sharpe ratio indicates better risk-adjusted performance, making it one of the most widely used metrics for comparing investment strategies.

### Sharpe Ratio vs. Sortino Ratio
While the Sharpe ratio penalizes all volatility—both upside and downside—the Sortino ratio focuses only on downside volatility. Because investors typically welcome upside surprises, the Sharpe ratio can sometimes overstate risk for strategies with positive skew. The Sortino ratio is often preferred when evaluating strategies where downside risk is the real concern.

### Size Factor
The size factor refers to the empirical tendency of small-capitalization stocks to outperform large-capitalization stocks over the long run. First documented in the Fama-French three-factor model, it suggests that companies with smaller market capitalizations generate higher risk-adjusted returns. The size premium is attributed to higher risk, lower liquidity, and greater neglected-firm effects.

### Sortino Ratio
The Sortino Ratio is a variation of the Sharpe ratio that uses downside deviation instead of total standard deviation as the measure of risk. It measures the return earned per unit of bad volatility—defined as the variability of returns below a minimum acceptable return, such as the risk-free rate. A higher Sortino ratio indicates better risk-adjusted performance against downside risk.

### Standard Deviation
Standard deviation is a statistical measure of the dispersion of returns around the mean, or average. It is the most common measure of total risk and is expressed in the same units as returns. In investing, higher standard deviation implies greater volatility and, assuming normal returns, a wider range of outcomes.

### Stochastic Oscillator
The stochastic oscillator is a momentum indicator that compares a particular closing price to a range of its prices over a certain period of time. It ranges from 0 to 100 and is used to identify overbought conditions above 80 and oversold conditions below 20. The indicator is based on the premise that prices tend to close near their highs in uptrends and near their lows in downtrends.

### Synergies (Hard / Soft)
In M&A, synergies are the expected financial benefits that arise when two companies combine. Hard synergies are quantifiable cost savings or revenue enhancements, such as headcount reductions or cross-selling opportunities. Soft synergies are less tangible and more difficult to verify, such as improved management, better culture, or strategic optionality.

## T

### Terminal Value
Terminal Value represents the value of a business beyond the explicit forecast period in a DCF model. It is calculated using either the perpetuity growth method or the exit multiple method. Because it often constitutes a large portion of total DCF value, small changes in terminal value assumptions can materially affect valuation outcomes.

### Tracking Error
Tracking Error measures the standard deviation of the difference between a portfolio's returns and its benchmark's returns. A high tracking error indicates a portfolio that deviates significantly from its benchmark, suggesting higher active risk. Low tracking error means the portfolio closely mirrors its index.

### Treynor Ratio
The Treynor Ratio measures return per unit of systematic risk, using beta instead of standard deviation. It is calculated as the portfolio excess return divided by its beta. Unlike the Sharpe ratio, which considers total risk, the Treynor ratio is best suited for evaluating well-diversified portfolios where unsystematic risk has been eliminated.

---

## U

### Upside Capture
Upside Capture measures how well a fund or portfolio performed relative to its benchmark during periods when the benchmark had positive returns. An upside capture ratio above 100% indicates the fund outperformed the benchmark in up markets. This metric helps investors understand which funds benefit most from bullish environments.

### Upside Potential
Upside potential refers to the possible gain from an investment, usually estimated by comparing current price to an analyst target or intrinsic value estimate. It is a forward-looking measure that helps investors assess the reward side of a risk-reward profile. Investments with higher upside potential typically carry proportionally higher risk.

---

## V

### Valuation
Valuation is the process of determining the current worth of an asset, security, or company. Common approaches include comparable company analysis, precedent transactions, and intrinsic methods such as DCF. Accurate valuation helps investors make informed buy, sell, or hold decisions based on the difference between intrinsic value and market price.

### Value at Risk (VaR)
VaR estimates the potential loss in value of a portfolio over a defined period for a given confidence interval. For example, a one-day 95% VaR of $1 million means there is a 5% chance of losing more than $1 million in one day. VaR is widely used for risk management but does not describe the magnitude of losses beyond the threshold.

### Value Factor
The value factor targets companies that trade at a discount relative to their fundamental worth, typically measured by metrics such as low P/E, low P/B, or high dividend yield. Value investing is based on the premise that markets overreact to bad news and underreact to good news, creating mispricings. The value factor has historically earned a risk premium, though it experiences long periods of underperformance.

### VaR (Value at Risk)
*See Value at Risk.*

### Variance
Variance is the average of the squared deviations from the mean and measures the dispersion of returns in a dataset. It is the square of standard deviation and is useful in mathematical models because variances can be added across uncorrelated sources of risk. High variance indicates greater uncertainty about future outcomes.

---

## W

### WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital)
WACC is the average rate of return a company is expected to pay to all its security holders—debt holders and equity investors. It is weighted by the proportion of debt and equity in the company's capital structure, with an adjustment for the tax deductibility of interest. WACC serves as the discount rate for DCF analyses and the hurdle rate for investment decisions.

### Working Capital
Working capital is the difference between current assets and current liabilities, reflecting the liquidity available to run day-to-day operations. Positive working capital means current assets exceed current liabilities, indicating the company can fund its short-term obligations. Insufficient working capital can lead to cash flow problems, while excessive working capital may indicate inefficiency.
